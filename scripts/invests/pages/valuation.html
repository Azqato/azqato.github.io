How it's calculated

  • Trailing P/E is the index's total market cap divided by its companies' net income over the last 12 months.
  • Forward P/E divides the same market cap by expected earnings: for each company, the average of analysts' estimates for this fiscal year and next.
  • Weighting: bigger companies count for more, as they do in the index. The Dow is price-weighted, so it adds up share prices and earnings per share instead.
  • Losses: every company's market cap counts, but only profits count as earnings. A company losing money adds to the price you pay and nothing to the earnings, the way most index providers treat it. Each row shows how many members are profitable.
  • Expected earnings growth is the trailing P/E divided by the forward P/E, minus 1. Because the forward estimate reaches into next year, it covers more than 12 months of growth.
  • Data: prices, market caps and earnings from Yahoo Finance, through the same weekly feed as the Screener. Members come from the S&P 500 and Nasdaq 100 lists, SSGA's DIA fund for the Dow, and Vanguard's VTWO fund for the Russell 2000. Updated every Monday, from Friday's close.

How it compares with published figures. Index P/Es vary by source, because each uses its own estimates and its own rules for losses and one-off gains. Checked against the Wall Street Journal's table in October 2026, these figures were within about 7% for the S&P 500, Nasdaq 100 and Dow. The Russell 2000 reads lower, by about a quarter on trailing and a third on forward: over 40% of its companies lose money, and in total its losses outweigh its profits, so its P/E depends almost entirely on how losses and one-off charges are treated.

Analysts' estimates tend to run optimistic, so the growth column is what they expect, not what will happen. Educational use only. Not financial advice.
